## The Big Idea

An Alt-Az mount has two axes: **altitude** (tilt up/down) and **azimuth** (rotate left/right). To track a star, both motors must run simultaneously at constantly varying speeds. The telescope stays level relative to the ground — intuitive for setup, but it causes the image to rotate over time.

## The Details

### Tracking Mechanics

As a star moves across the sky, the mount must:

- **Azimuth axis**: rotates to follow horizontal motion (fastest near the horizon)
- **Altitude axis**: tilts to follow vertical motion (fastest near the meridian)

Both axes must be controlled simultaneously with varying speeds — a complex kinematic problem the mount's firmware solves in real time.

### Advantages

- No polar alignment needed (just level the base)
- Compact and lightweight
- Easy to set up anywhere
- Wide field of view even at low altitudes
- Perfect for grab-and-go smart telescopes

### Disadvantages

- **Field rotation** — the image rotates over time
- Limits sub-exposure to 10–30 seconds (depending on declination and latitude)
- Requires complex 2-axis tracking algorithms
- Cannot do traditional long-exposure astrophotography

---

## In STAIRS

STAIRS respects the 10–20s sub-exposure limit of Alt-Az mounts when computing **total integration time**. It also accounts for the **meridian penalty** — targets near the meridian have different tracking characteristics that affect image quality.

### Tracking Rate Equations

An Alt-Az mount must track a star at these rates:

$$\dot{A} = \frac{15 \cos(\delta) \cos(H)}{\cos^2(\delta) \sin^2(H) + \cos^2(a)}$$

$$\dot{a} = -15 \cos(\delta) \sin(H)$$

Where $A$ = azimuth, $a$ = altitude, $\delta$ = declination, $H$ = hour angle. The rates are not constant — they change continuously.

At culmination (meridian crossing), the azimuth rate changes sign abruptly, requiring a rapid reversal. This is why tracking often glitches near the meridian.
