## The Big Idea

A single short exposure of a faint nebula looks like random noise — you can barely see anything. But the **signal** (the nebula) is the same in every frame, while the **noise** is random and different each time. By averaging many frames together, the signal adds up while the noise averages toward zero. This is **stacking**.

## The Details

### Why It Works

Each frame contains:

- **Signal (S)**: light from the target — the same in every frame
- **Noise (N)**: random fluctuations — different in every frame

When you average $n$ frames:

- Signal: $S \times n$ (adds linearly)
- Noise: $\sqrt{N_1^2 + N_2^2 + ...} \approx \sqrt{n} \times N$ (adds in quadrature)

SNR improvement: $\text{SNR}_n = \sqrt{n} \times \text{SNR}_1$

**To double SNR, you need 4× the frames.** To improve SNR by 10×, you need 100× the frames. This is the fundamental law of astrophotography — there's no shortcut.

### The Analogy

Imagine trying to hear a whisper in a crowded room:

- One listen: you barely hear it over the noise
- Listen 100 times and average: the whisper becomes clear because the random noise cancels out

Stacking does the same for light.

---

## In STAIRS

The $\sqrt{n}$ relationship is central to STAIRS's planning engine. Given a target brightness, sky conditions, and sensor properties, STAIRS computes:

- How many frames are needed to reach a target SNR (typically SNR = 10)
- How much total integration time that requires
- Whether the target is feasible in a single night

<DeepDive title="Deep Dive: The Root-N Law">

### Mathematical Proof

Let each frame have signal $S$ and noise $\sigma$. After stacking $n$ frames:

$$S_{\text{stack}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} S_i = nS$$

$$\sigma_{\text{stack}} = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \sigma_i^2} = \sqrt{n}\sigma$$

$$\text{SNR}_{\text{stack}} = \frac{nS}{\sqrt{n}\sigma} = \sqrt{n} \cdot \frac{S}{\sigma} = \sqrt{n} \cdot \text{SNR}_1$$

The improvement scales as $\sqrt{n}$. This assumes uncorrelated noise — which is approximately true for shot noise (the dominant noise source in astrophotography).

### The Cost of Dimness

If the per-frame SNR is very low (< 1), the target is effectively invisible in individual frames. But stacking can still recover it:

| Per-frame SNR | Frames needed for SNR=5 | Frames needed for SNR=10 |
| ------------- | ----------------------- | ------------------------ |
| 0.1           | 2,500                   | 10,000                   |
| 0.2           | 625                     | 2,500                    |
| 0.5           | 100                     | 400                      |
| 1.0           | 25                      | 100                      |
| 2.0           | 7                       | 25                       |

This table explains why smart telescopes need many frames. A typical 10-second Alt-Az sub might have SNR ≈ 0.3–0.5 on a faint nebula, requiring hundreds of frames.
