## The Big Idea

Your camera sensor has imperfections: some pixels are more sensitive than others, some always read high (hot pixels), and dust on the optics casts shadows. **Calibration frames** are special exposures that map these imperfections so they can be mathematically removed from your science frames.

## The Details

### The Three Types

**Dark frames** (same exposure time, same temperature as lights):

- Capture thermal noise (dark current)
- Hot pixels and amp glow
- Subtracted from light frames
- Take 10–20 at the end of the session

**Flat frames** (uniformly illuminated, short exposure):

- Capture vignetting (darker corners)
- Dust shadows
- Divided into light frames
- Take at twilight (20–30 frames)

**Bias frames** (shortest possible exposure):

- Capture read noise pattern
- Subtracted from darks and flats
- Take 50–100 once per session

### The Math

$$I_{\text{calibrated}} = \frac{I_{\text{raw}} - I_{\text{dark}}}{I_{\text{flat}} - I_{\text{bias}}}$$

Where each term is a **master** frame (median or average of many individual calibration frames). _Note: Depending on the camera and exposure time, Flats sometimes require their own "Flat-Darks" to be subtracted instead of Biases._

### Smart Telescopes and Calibration

Most smart telescopes handle calibration internally:

- **Dark frames**: some take them automatically at startup
- **Flat frames**: some use built-in LED panels or take them at twilight
- **Bias frames**: built into the sensor's reference

The quality of the internal calibration varies between models. Some handle it transparently, others may not do any calibration at all.

---

## In STAIRS

STAIRS doesn't handle calibration frames directly (that's the telescope's firmware), but the quality of calibration affects the **achievable SNR**. Better calibration = less fixed pattern noise = closer to the theoretical $\sqrt{n}$ stacking improvement.
