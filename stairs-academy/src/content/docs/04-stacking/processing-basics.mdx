## The Big Idea

When you open a raw astronomical image, it often looks completely black. This is because astronomical data is **linear** — most of the signal is bunched up in the darkest 1% of the range. To see the nebula, we must "stretch" the data, re-mapping those dark values to a visible range.

## The Details

### Linear vs. Non-linear

Our eyes and standard cameras are non-linear; they apply a curve to make images look natural. Astronomical sensors are **linear counters**: if a pixel receives twice as many photons, it records twice the value.

- **Linear Data**: Preserves the physical relationship between light and measurement. Essential for science and stacking.
- **Non-linear Data**: Stretched for display. This is what you see in a finished JPG.

### The Histogram

A histogram is a graph showing how many pixels have each brightness value. In a raw astronomical image:

1. There is a huge spike near the left (the dark sky background).
2. The nebula signal is a tiny "hump" just to the right of the background spike.
3. The rest of the graph is empty.

### The Stretching Process

1. **Black Point**: We move the "zero" value up to the edge of the sky background spike. This removes the grey haze of light pollution.
2. **Midtones (Gamma)**: We apply a curve that lifts the faint nebula signal without blowing out the bright stars.
3. **White Point**: We set the maximum brightness level. Usually, we leave this alone to avoid "clipping" (losing detail in) the bright centers of stars.

---

## In STAIRS

Understanding stretching is vital for evaluating **Surface Brightness**. STAIRS calculates scores based on the physical (linear) signal, but you judge the result based on the stretched image. A target with low surface brightness will require a much more aggressive stretch, which also makes the noise more visible.

<DeepDive title="Deep Dive: The Math of Stretching">

### Power Law Stretch (Gamma)

The most basic stretch is a power law:

$$V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}}^{\gamma}$$

Where $\gamma < 1$ (typically 0.2 to 0.5) to brighten midtones.

### ArcSinh Stretch

A more advanced stretch used in professional software like PixInsight is the **Inverse Hyperbolic Sine (ArcSinh)** stretch. Its unique property is that it preserves color ratios while stretching intensity:

$$f(x) = \frac{\text{asinh}(x \cdot \beta)}{\text{asinh}(\beta)}$$

Where $\beta$ is a stretching factor. Unlike a standard gamma stretch, ArcSinh doesn't "wash out" colors as the image gets brighter.
