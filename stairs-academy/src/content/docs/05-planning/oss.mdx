## The Big Idea

Not every target works with every telescope. A huge nebula like the Heart Nebula is too big for the Seestar S50's field of view — you'd only see a small piece. A tiny galaxy looks beautiful but only covers a few pixels. The **Object Suitability Score** (OSS) quantifies this match.

## The Details

### OSS = FOV Fit (40%) + Surface Brightness (30%) + Altitude (30%)

### FOV Fit

Targets come in different angular sizes. A galaxy might be 10 arcminutes across; a nebula might be 60 arcminutes. Your sensor has a fixed field of view (e.g., the Seestar S50 sees 77 × 43 arcminutes).

- **Target too small**: occupies only a few pixels — not enough detail (score penalty)
- **Target too big**: doesn't fit on the sensor (score penalty)
- **Just right**: fills a good portion of the frame (score = 100)

The score follows a **Gaussian decay** centered on the ideal target size (about 30–40% of the sensor's shorter dimension).

### Surface Brightness

Surface brightness measures how concentrated the target's light is:

$$\text{SB} = \text{mag} + 2.5 \times \log_{10}(\text{angular area})$$

A magnitude 8 star has SB ≈ 8 (tiny area). A magnitude 8 nebula spread over 30 arcminutes has SB ≈ 18 (much dimmer per pixel).

Higher SB = fainter = harder to image = lower score.

### Altitude Score

Targets near the horizon:

- Light travels through more atmosphere (more extinction)
- More atmospheric dispersion (color fringing)
- More likely to be blocked by trees/buildings/horizon
- Shorter time available before they set

Targets near zenith (> 75°):

- Awkward for Alt-Az tracking
- Increased field rotation rate
- Penalty applied

The ideal altitude range is 30–75°.

## In STAIRS

OSS is computed once per target (it doesn't depend on the date or weather). STAIRS pre-computes OSS for all targets in your configured catalogs, then applies the dynamic SQS multiplier when you request a plan.
