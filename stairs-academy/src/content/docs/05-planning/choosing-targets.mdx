## The Big Idea

Not all targets are created equal. A bright star cluster can be imaged from the middle of a city, while a faint galaxy might require a two-hour drive to a dark mountain site. Successful planning starts with choosing the right target for your specific environment.

---

## Target Categories

### 1. Star Clusters (Bortle 1–9)

- **Examples**: M13 (Hercules Cluster), M45 (The Pleiades).
- **Why**: They are bright, point-like, and highly concentrated.
- **Strategy**: Excellent for beginners and city observers. They look great even in short integration times.

### 2. Emission Nebulae (Bortle 1–8 with filters)

- **Examples**: M42 (Orion), NGC 7000 (North American).
- **Why**: They emit light at specific wavelengths (Hα, OIII).
- **Strategy**: Use a **Narrowband (Dual-Band) filter**. This allows you to image them effectively even from light-polluted backyards.

### 3. Galaxies (Bortle 1–4 preferred)

- **Examples**: M31 (Andromeda), M51 (Whirlpool).
- **Why**: They are "broadband" targets — they emit light across the whole spectrum.
- **Strategy**: Very difficult in cities because light pollution washes out the faint outer arms. Best saved for dark sites.

## The STAIRS Approach

STAIRS automates this decision-making process through the **Absolute Quality Score (AQS)**. It penalizes diffuse, broadband targets in city locations while rewarding targets that "fit" your conditions.

**When evaluating recommendations, look for:**

- **High OSS**: The target fits your telescope's field of view.
- **High SQS**: The sky is clear, and the moon is away.
- **High AQS**: The net result is a high-quality imaging opportunity.

<DeepDive title="Deep Dive: Contrast vs. Brightness">

### The Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) Limit

In a city (Bortle 8), the "noise" from the sky background can be 100× brighter than the "signal" from a faint galaxy arm. Even with stacking, the amount of time required to see the signal increases exponentially:

$$t_{\text{needed}} \propto \frac{1}{\text{Contrast}^2}$$

If you move from a city (Contrast = 0.1) to a dark site (Contrast = 1.0), you need **100× less time** to reach the same image quality. This is why "dark site time" is the most valuable resource in astrophotography.
