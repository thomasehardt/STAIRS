## The Big Idea

A telescope is just a bucket for light. Your eye's pupil is about 5mm at night. A typical smart telescope has a 50mm aperture — that's **100x more light** ([explanation](../deep-dive-aperture#aperture-light-gathering-power)). That's the whole game: collect more photons, see fainter things.

Everything else — magnification, resolution, tracking — exists to support this one job.

## The Details

### Aperture Is Everything

The **aperture** is the diameter of the main light-collecting element (lens or mirror). It determines how many photons per second the telescope captures.

Bigger aperture = more light = shorter exposures = fainter objects visible.

**Key terms:**

- **Aperture (D):** diameter in mm — 50mm on most smart telescopes
- **Focal length (f):** distance the light travels inside the telescope before coming to focus
- **Focal ratio (f/D):** f-number, like a camera lens — f/5 means focal length is 5× the aperture

### The Resolution Limit

A bigger aperture doesn't just collect more light — it also resolves finer detail. The theoretical limit is:

```
resolution (arcseconds) ≈ 116 / D(mm)
```

For a 50mm telescope, that's about 2.3 arcseconds. For reference, a full moon is 1800 arcseconds across.

## In STAIRS

When you configure a telescope profile in STAIRS, the aperture and focal length drive:

- **Field of view** calculation — how much sky fits on your sensor
- **Pixel scale** — arcseconds per pixel (determines sampling)
- **Light collection rate** — used to estimate required exposure time

### How Many Photons?

A 0th-magnitude star delivers about:

$$F_0 \approx 10^{10} \ \text{photons} / \text{m}^2 / \text{s}$$

through a standard photometric band. A telescope of aperture $D$ collects:

$$F_{\text{tel}} = F_0 \cdot \frac{\pi D^2}{4} \cdot 10^{-0.4m} \cdot QE \cdot T$$

Where:

- $m$ = apparent magnitude of the target
- $QE$ = quantum efficiency of the sensor (0–1)
- $T$ = transmission of the optics (typically 0.7–0.9)

For a magnitude 10 target with a 50mm telescope at QE=0.7:

$$F_{\text{tel}} \approx 10^{10} \cdot \frac{\pi (0.05)^2}{4} \cdot 10^{-0.4 \cdot 10} \cdot 0.7 \approx 5500 \ \text{photons/s}$$

That's spread across the sensor's pixels, so each pixel gets a fraction.

### Why Magnitude Is Logarithmic

A difference of 5 magnitudes = exactly 100× in brightness. Each magnitude step is:

$$\frac{I_1}{I_2} = 10^{-0.4(m_1 - m_2)}$$

So a magnitude 5 star is 100× brighter than a magnitude 10 star.
