## The Big Idea

There are two basic ways to focus light: bend it with a lens (refractor) or bounce it off a mirror (reflector). Most smart telescopes use a **catadioptric** design — a mix of both — to pack a long focal length into a short tube.

## The Details

### Refractor

- Uses a glass lens at the front to bend light
- Simple, sealed design (no dust on optics)
- Suffers from **chromatic aberration** — different colors focus at different points
- Used in the **Dwarf II** (wide-angle camera)

### Reflector

- Uses a curved mirror at the back to focus light
- No chromatic aberration (mirrors reflect all colors equally)
- Can be made larger for less money
- Requires occasional collimation (alignment of mirrors)

### Catadioptric (Smart Telescopes)

- Uses both lenses and mirrors
- "Folds" the light path — long focal length in a short tube
- Corrects aberrations with built-in lens elements
- Most common design in smart telescopes (Seestar S50, Vespera)

## In STAIRS

The optical design determines the **focal length** and **aperture**, which are the two most important parameters in the telescope profile. STAIRS uses these to compute everything else.

| Telescope       | Aperture | Focal Length | f/ratio | Design       |
| --------------- | -------- | ------------ | ------- | ------------ |
| Seestar S50     | 50mm     | 250mm        | f/5     | Catadioptric |
| Dwarf II (tele) | 24mm     | 86mm         | f/3.6   | Refractor    |
| Vespera         | 50mm     | 250mm        | f/5     | Catadioptric |

### The Light Cone

Light from a distant object enters the telescope as parallel rays. The lens or mirror bends these rays to converge at the focal plane. The **focal ratio** (f-number) describes how steep this cone is:

$$f/\# = \frac{f}{D}$$

A faster f-ratio (lower number) means:

- Steeper light cone
- Shorter exposure for same brightness
- Wider field of view
- More demanding on sensor alignment

The image surface brightness at the focal plane depends only on f-ratio, not aperture:

$$I_{\text{focal}} \propto \frac{1}{(f/\#)^2}$$

This is why f/5 is f/5 regardless of whether it's a 50mm or 500mm telescope — but the larger aperture collects more total light (over a wider field).
