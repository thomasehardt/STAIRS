## The Big Idea

The atmosphere is the final "lens" in your optical system, and unfortunately, it's a very poor one. Even with the best telescope in the world, the moving air above you determines how sharp your images will be (**Seeing**) and how much light actually reaches your sensor (**Transparency**).

## The Details

### Seeing (Stability)

"Seeing" refers to the stability of the air. When air layers with different temperatures mix, they refract light in random directions.

- **Good Seeing**: The air is still. Stars are pinpoint dots. You can resolve fine detail on planets and galaxies.
- **Poor Seeing**: The air is turbulent. Stars "twinkle" and appear as bloated, boiling blobs. High-resolution detail is washed out.

Seeing is measured in arcseconds (FWHM - Full Width at Half Maximum). Most locations have seeing between 1.5" and 3.0".

### Transparency (Clarity)

Transparency is a measure of how clear the air is — specifically, how much light is blocked by water vapor, dust, or smoke.

- **High Transparency**: The sky is "inky black." Faint stars are easy to see.
- **Low Transparency**: The sky looks milky or hazy. Even if it's "clear" (no clouds), the signal from your target is being absorbed by the haze.

### Atmospheric Extinction

As you look closer to the horizon, you are looking through more atmosphere. This is called **airmass**.

- At **90° (Zenith)**, you are looking through 1.0 airmass.
- At **30° altitude**, you are looking through 2.0 airmasses.
- At **15° altitude**, you are looking through 3.9 airmasses.

More airmass means more light is absorbed (extinction) and more turbulence (poorer seeing). This is why STAIRS penalizes targets below 30°.

### Reading a Forecast

Astronomy-specific forecasts report seeing and transparency as separate indices — treat them as two different questions, not one "is it clear tonight" answer:

- **Seeing index**: usually derived from upper-level wind shear and jet stream models. Jet stream speeds above roughly 80 knots reliably predict degraded seeing, even under a cloud-free sky.
- **Transparency index**: derived from humidity and modeled aerosol/dust content. A small gap between air temperature and dew point (high relative humidity) predicts haze even when cloud cover shows 0%.

A cloud-free forecast is necessary but not sufficient — always check both indices, since a hazy-but-still night and a clear-but-jet-stream-battered night fail for opposite reasons.

## In STAIRS

The atmosphere is a key component of the **Sky Quality Score (SQS)**:

- **Seeing Multiplier**: If forecasted seeing is > 4.0", a penalty is applied because detail will be lost.
- **Humidity Multiplier**: High humidity often correlates with poor transparency and increased risk of dew.
- **Altitude Trace**: STAIRS tracks the target's altitude throughout the night to ensure you image it when airmass is lowest.

### Turbulence Physics

The quality of seeing is physically described by the **Fried parameter** ($r_0$). It represents the diameter of a telescope that would have the same resolution as a perfect telescope limited by the atmosphere.

$$\theta_{\text{seeing}} \approx 0.98 \frac{\lambda}{r_0}$$

If $r_0 = 10\text{cm}$, your 50mm telescope is "diffraction limited" (the telescope is the bottleneck). If $r_0 = 3\text{cm}$ (very poor seeing), the atmosphere is the bottleneck, and even a small smart telescope will show bloated stars.

### Scintillation

Twinkling (scintillation) is caused by small-scale density fluctuations in the upper atmosphere. It primarily affects the _brightness_ of the star, while seeing affects its _position and shape_. For long exposures, scintillation averages out, but seeing remains as a constant blur.
