## The Big Idea

Light behaves as both a wave and a particle. In astrophotography, we care about the particle picture: light arrives as individual **photons**, each carrying a discrete amount of energy. A dim object sends fewer photons per second. A bright object sends more. That's it.

---

## The Details

### Photon Basics

- A photon is a quantum of electromagnetic energy
- Its energy depends on its wavelength (color): $E = hc / \lambda$
- Blue photons carry more energy than red photons
- The human eye can detect individual photons under ideal conditions
- A camera sensor counts photons and records the tally

### Why Photons Matter for Astrophotography

Most astrophotography targets are **extremely dim** — few photons per pixel per second. This means:

- **Shot noise**: photon arrival is random (Poisson statistics). The uncertainty is $\sqrt{N}$ where $N$ is the number of photons detected.
- **SNR**: signal-to-noise ratio = $N / \sqrt{N} = \sqrt{N}$. More photons = better image.
- **Low signal**: a faint nebula might deliver only 1–2 photons per pixel per second.

### Flux and Magnitude

Astronomers measure brightness using **magnitude**, a logarithmic scale:

- Lower magnitude = brighter
- A difference of 5 magnitudes = exactly 100× in brightness
- The Sun: -26.7, Full Moon: -12.6, Venus: -4.4, Vega: 0.0
- Faintest star visible in 50mm telescope: ~+10.5

The photon flux from a star of magnitude $m$ is:

$$F = F_0 \cdot 10^{-0.4m}$$

Where $F_0$ is the flux of a magnitude 0 star (about $10^{10}$ photons/m²/s in visible light).

---

## In STAIRS

STAIRS uses target magnitude as one input to its scoring system. The **Surface Brightness Score** accounts for how bright the target appears per unit area — a diffuse nebula of magnitude 10 is much harder to image than a star of magnitude 10, because those photons are spread over a larger area.

### How a Sensor Detects Photons

When a photon hits silicon, it can knock an electron loose — this is the **photoelectric effect**. The electron is collected in a **potential well** (essentially a tiny bucket). After the exposure, the charge in each well is measured and converted to a digital number.

Key constraints:

- **Quantum Efficiency (QE)**: not every photon creates an electron. Typical QE for CMOS sensors is 50–80%.
- **Full Well Capacity**: each well can hold a limited number of electrons (typically 10,000–100,000). When full, the pixel saturates.
- **Dark Current**: electrons generated by heat, not light. Cooling the sensor reduces this exponentially.
- **Read Noise**: the electronic noise introduced when reading out the charge. Typically 1–5 electrons for modern CMOS sensors.

The total signal in electrons per pixel is:

$$S = F \cdot A \cdot QE \cdot t$$

Where $F$ = photon flux (photons/m²/s), $A$ = pixel area on the sky, $QE$ = quantum efficiency, $t$ = exposure time.
