## The Big Idea

Most light pollution is "broadband" — it covers the whole rainbow. But many nebulae emit light at very specific wavelengths (colors) when their gases are excited by nearby stars. **Narrowband filters** act like a keyhole, blocking all light _except_ those specific emission lines, allowing you to see nebulae even from bright cities.

## The Details

### Common Emission Lines

1. **Hydrogen-Alpha (Hα)**: 656.3 nm (Deep Red). The most common emission in the universe. Most nebulae are primarily Hα.
2. **Oxygen-III (OIII)**: 500.7 nm (Teal/Blue-Green). Emitted by doubly ionized oxygen. Common in planetary nebulae and supernova remnants.
3. **Sulfur-II (SII)**: 671.6 nm (Deep Red). Traces shocks and high-energy regions.

### Dual-Band Filters (HOO)

Many smart telescopes use "Dual-Band" filters that pass both Hα and OIII simultaneously. Since Hα falls in the Red channel and OIII falls in the Green and Blue channels of your color sensor, you get a full-color image of the nebula in a single exposure.

### The Hubble Palette (SHO)

To see the structure of different gases, scientists often map these three lines to the standard Red, Green, and Blue channels:

- **Sulfur-II** → Red
- **Hα** → Green
- **Oxygen-III** → Blue

This creates the iconic "golden and blue" look made famous by the Hubble Space Telescope.

## In STAIRS

Narrowband filters change the **Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)** calculation significantly:

- **Signal**: Slightly reduced (filter absorption).
- **Noise**: Massively reduced (blocks 95%+ of light pollution).
- **Result**: A huge net increase in contrast, allowing much higher "Absolute Quality Scores" from city locations.

### Bandpass & FWHM

The "narrowness" of a filter is measured by its **FWHM (Full Width at Half Maximum)**.

- **Broadband**: 100nm+
- **Standard Narrowband**: 7nm - 12nm
- **Ultra-Narrow**: 3nm - 5nm

The narrower the filter, the better it rejects light pollution, but the more expensive it becomes and the more precisely it must be manufactured.

### Filter Shift (f/ratio)

As the light cone becomes steeper (faster f/ratio like f/2), the angle of incidence on the filter changes. This causes the bandpass to **shift** toward blue wavelengths. Fast telescopes require "pre-shifted" filters to ensure the Hα line still lands in the center of the transmission window.
