Every term below is explained in full where it first comes up in the curriculum — this page exists so you don't have to go hunting for a definition mid-lesson. Each entry is deliberately short; follow the link if you want the full picture.

## Light & Optics

- **Aperture** — the diameter of a telescope's main light-collecting lens or mirror. Bigger aperture means more light collected per second, and fainter targets become visible. See [Telescopes Are Light Buckets](/stairs-docs/01-telescopes/light-buckets/).
- **Focal length** — the distance light travels inside the telescope before coming to a focus. Combined with aperture, it sets the focal ratio.
- **Focal ratio (f-number)** — focal length divided by aperture (like a camera's f-stop). A lower number means a brighter image per pixel and a wider field of view, at the cost of magnification.
- **Photon** — a single discrete packet of light energy. Astrophotography is fundamentally about counting these one at a time. See [Light as Photons](/stairs-docs/02-photon-pixel/light-as-photons/).
- **Magnitude** — the astronomical brightness scale, where _lower_ numbers are brighter and each step of 5 magnitudes equals exactly 100x in brightness.
- **Chromatic aberration** — the tendency of a simple lens to focus different colors of light at slightly different points, blurring the image. Mirrors don't have this problem.
- **Central obstruction** — the small mirror or baffle blocking part of a telescope's aperture in a folded (catadioptric) design, trading a modest amount of light loss for a much shorter tube.

## Atmosphere

- **Seeing** — how much the atmosphere's turbulence blurs an image, measured in arcseconds (FWHM). Bad seeing makes stars look bloated even on an otherwise clear night. See [The Atmosphere](/stairs-docs/02-photon-pixel/the-atmosphere/).
- **Transparency** — how much haze, humidity, or aerosols dim and scatter light, independent of turbulence. Bad transparency buries faint targets in noise without necessarily blurring anything.
- **Airmass / extinction** — the amount of atmosphere a line of sight passes through. Looking near the horizon means more airmass, more extinction, and worse seeing.
- **FWHM (Full Width at Half Maximum)** — the standard way of measuring how "fat" a blurred star's profile is; the unit seeing is quoted in.

## Sensors

- **CMOS sensor** — the grid of light-sensitive pixels that converts photons into an electronic, then digital, image. See [Sensor Capture](/stairs-docs/02-photon-pixel/sensor-capture/).
- **Quantum Efficiency (QE)** — the percentage of incoming photons that a pixel successfully converts into a stored electron. Higher is better; 50-80% is typical for modern sensors.
- **Full well capacity** — the maximum number of electrons a single pixel can hold before it saturates (clips to pure white).
- **Read noise** — the small, fixed amount of uncertainty added every time a pixel is read out, regardless of exposure length.
- **Dark current (thermal noise)** — a trickle of electrons generated by heat alone, with no light involved. It roughly doubles for every 6-8°C rise in sensor temperature.
- **Shot noise** — the natural randomness in when photons arrive, following Poisson statistics. Cannot be engineered away — it's a property of light itself.
- **Gain** — the conversion factor between electrons and the digital number a pixel ends up with. Raising gain doesn't add photons; it just repositions where your existing signal and noise sit in the digital range.
- **Photoelectric effect** — the physical process where a photon striking silicon knocks an electron loose, which is what a sensor actually detects.

## Filters & Color

- **Bayer filter** — the fixed red/green/blue mosaic laid over a color sensor's pixels, since no pixel can distinguish color on its own. See [Color & the Bayer Filter](/stairs-docs/02-photon-pixel/bayer-filter/).
- **Bortle Scale** — a 1-to-9 sky-darkness rating, from pristine (1) to inner-city (9), based on naked-eye limiting magnitude and visible sky features.
- **Narrowband filter** — a filter passing only a few-nanometer-wide window around a specific emission wavelength (like Ha), rejecting most broadband light pollution. See [Narrowband Imaging](/stairs-docs/02-photon-pixel/narrowband-imaging/).
- **Dual-band filter** — a narrowband filter passing two emission lines at once (typically Ha and OIII), letting a one-shot color camera capture both in a single exposure.
- **Hubble Palette (SHO)** — the convention of mapping SII/Ha/OIII narrowband data to red/green/blue display channels for maximum visual contrast — a deliberate false-color choice, not a natural one.
- **True color vs. false color** — true color approximates what the eye would see; false color (like SHO) deliberately reassigns wavelengths to different display colors for scientific or aesthetic contrast.

## The Sky's Coordinate System

- **Right Ascension (RA) / Declination (Dec)** — the sky's equivalent of longitude and latitude, fixed to the star field rather than to any one observer. See [The Celestial Coordinate System](/stairs-docs/03-mounts/celestial-coordinates/).
- **Epoch (e.g., J2000.0)** — the reference date a set of RA/Dec coordinates assumes, needed because of slow precession drift.
- **Precession** — the ~26,000-year wobble of Earth's rotational axis, which very slowly shifts every coordinate over time.
- **Ephemeris** — a table or calculator that outputs a moving object's (comet, asteroid) position for a specific date and time, since a single coordinate goes stale quickly.
- **Plate solving** — the computer-vision technique of matching a star field's geometric pattern against a catalog to determine exactly where a telescope is pointed. See [Plate Solving](/stairs-docs/04-stacking/plate-solving/).

## Mounts & Tracking

- **Alt-Az mount** — a telescope mount with two axes (altitude and azimuth) aligned to the local horizon. Simple and compact, but causes field rotation. See [Why Mounts Matter](/stairs-docs/03-mounts/why-mounts-matter/).
- **Equatorial (EQ) mount** — a mount with one axis tilted to align with Earth's rotational axis, eliminating field rotation at the cost of requiring polar alignment.
- **Polar alignment** — pointing an EQ mount's RA axis at the celestial pole so its single tracking axis exactly cancels Earth's rotation.
- **Field rotation** — the apparent rotation of the image around a tracked target, caused by an Alt-Az mount's axes not lining up with the sky's own rotation axis.
- **Sidereal rate** — the speed Earth actually rotates relative to the stars (15.041 arcseconds/second) — slightly faster than the 24-hour solar day.
- **Autoguiding** — using a secondary camera to monitor a guide star and send small real-time tracking corrections.

## Stacking & Processing

- **Stacking** — combining many exposures so the constant signal reinforces itself while random noise averages toward zero. See [Why Stack Images](/stairs-docs/04-stacking/why-stack/).
- **SNR (Signal-to-Noise Ratio)** — a measure of how much real signal you have relative to noise; improving it by 10x requires roughly 100x the total exposure time.
- **Dithering** — deliberately nudging the telescope a few pixels between frames so fixed sensor defects (hot pixels) land on different sky positions and average out during stacking.
- **Calibration frames (dark / flat / bias)** — special exposures used to mathematically remove a sensor's own imperfections (thermal noise, vignetting, electronic offset) from the real image.
- **Sigma clipping** — a statistical method for rejecting outlier pixels (satellite trails, cosmic rays) before averaging a stack together.
- **Stretching** — remapping a linear image's brightness values non-linearly so faint signal buried near black becomes visible on a normal display.

## Targets & Distance

- **Emission nebula (HII region)** — a cloud of hydrogen gas glowing because nearby young, hot stars are ionizing it — a sign of active star formation. See [Stellar Evolution and the Cosmic Distance Scale](/stairs-docs/05-planning/target-types-and-distances/).
- **Planetary nebula** — the glowing shell of gas shed by a dying Sun-like star, ionized by the exposed white dwarf core left behind.
- **Supernova remnant** — the expanding, shock-heated debris from a massive star's explosive death.
- **Standard candle** — an object (like a specific type of supernova) whose true brightness is known well enough to calculate distance from how faint it appears.
- **Redshift** — the stretching of light's wavelength caused by the expansion of space, used to estimate distances to the most remote galaxies.

## STAIRS Scoring

- **Object Suitability Score (OSS)** — how well a target fits your specific telescope, independent of tonight's conditions. See [Object Suitability Score](/stairs-docs/05-planning/oss/).
- **Sky Quality Score (SQS)** — a 0-1 multiplier capturing tonight's weather, moon, and altitude conditions for a specific target.
- **Absolute Quality Score (AQS)** — OSS combined with SQS and the Bortle multiplier, giving a final, night-specific ranking.
- **Meridian penalty** — a scoring penalty applied to targets very close to the meridian, where field rotation and tracking challenges are greatest.
